# Positioning Framework

Use this framework to evaluate whether a startup has a clear, defensible, buyer-ready position in the market. Apply this before writing a single word of copy critique — weak positioning cannot be fixed with better headlines.

---

## 1. Category clarity

**What to evaluate:** Does the visitor immediately know what category this company belongs to? Can they place it in their mental map within 3 seconds?

**Questions to ask:**
- What category does the company appear to occupy? (e.g., "AI consulting agency", "workflow automation platform", "AI training provider")
- Is the category one the buyer already understands, or is the company trying to create a new one?
- Does the category signal who the buyer is and what problem gets solved?
- Is the company accidentally straddling multiple categories, creating confusion?

**Common failure modes:**
- "We're a technology partner for digital transformation" — category unclear, too broad
- Listing 6 different services with no unifying category label
- Using emerging jargon the ICP doesn't use yet ("agentic AI platform" when buyers say "automation tools")
- Being in a crowded category without differentiation (everyone says "AI for business")

**What good looks like:**
- "AI implementation agency for mid-market manufacturing companies in Latin America"
- The category is narrow enough to be believable, broad enough to not be a niche

---

## 2. Target customer

**What to evaluate:** Is there a specific, named type of person or company this is built for? Specific beats broad.

**Questions to ask:**
- Who is the named buyer? (job title, company type, company size)
- Is this B2B or B2C? What industry? What geography?
- Is the targeting specific enough that a buyer self-identifies and a non-buyer self-disqualifies?
- Does the copy speak to that person's specific situation, or is it generic?

**Common failure modes:**
- "For any company looking to innovate with AI" — targets nobody specifically
- Listing 5 different verticals without prioritizing
- "Enterprises and SMEs" — mutually exclusive buying motions
- LATAM-focused company using US startup jargon without translation

**What good looks like:**
- "For operations and IT teams at 100–1000 person companies in Brazil and Mexico who are starting their AI adoption journey"

---

## 3. Main pain

**What to evaluate:** Is the #1 pain the company addresses stated explicitly? Implied pain is invisible to buyers.

**Questions to ask:**
- What exact problem does the buyer have before they find this company?
- Is the pain stated in the buyer's language, or the founder's language?
- Is the pain acute (they're feeling it right now) or latent (they don't know they have it)?
- Does the hero section name the pain, or does it skip to the solution?

**Common failure modes:**
- Opening with "We help companies leverage AI" — skips the pain entirely
- Describing the solution without establishing why the buyer should care
- Pain is too abstract ("inefficiency", "digital transformation challenges")
- Pain is real but not urgent enough to trigger buying behavior

**What good looks like:**
- "Your team is drowning in manual data processing. AI tools exist, but nobody knows how to implement them without breaking existing workflows."

---

## 4. Transformation promised

**What to evaluate:** Is there a clear before/after? Does the buyer know their world looks different after working with this company?

**Questions to ask:**
- What does the buyer's life look like AFTER? Is this stated explicitly?
- Is the transformation specific enough to be credible?
- Is the transformation differentiated from what a competitor would promise?
- Does the transformation match the category and ICP?

**Common failure modes:**
- "Accelerate your AI journey" — transformation vague
- Before/after not stated; buyer must infer it
- Transformation sounds the same as every competitor's promise
- Over-claiming ("Transform your entire business") with no specifics

**What good looks like:**
- "Within 90 days, your team processes vendor invoices 80% faster using an AI agent you own and can maintain."

---

## 5. Urgency

**What to evaluate:** Why should the buyer act now rather than wait 6 months?

**Questions to ask:**
- Is there a credible reason to act now stated anywhere?
- Is urgency created by external forces (competitors adopting AI, regulation, market shift) or internal pressure?
- Does the company manufacture urgency (artificial scarcity, fake deadlines) or earn it?

**Common failure modes:**
- No urgency — the site implies they can come back whenever
- "Spots are limited" without any context — buyer won't believe it
- Urgency is present but not tied to the buyer's specific situation
- AI companies often miss the "your competitors are already doing this" angle

---

## 6. Differentiation

**What to evaluate:** Why this company instead of the 10 alternatives? Is it stated or assumed?

**Questions to ask:**
- What is the single most differentiated thing about this company?
- Is differentiation stated in the hero, or buried on an "About" page?
- Is differentiation real (unique capability, proprietary methodology, exclusive access) or claimed (better, faster, smarter)?
- Can a competitor copy this claim in a single afternoon?

**Common failure modes:**
- "We're different because we care more" — not differentiation
- Differentiation based entirely on being local/regional without explaining why that matters to outcomes
- Expertise claims without evidence ("10 years experience")
- No differentiation statement at all

**What good looks like:**
- "We're the only AI implementation firm in LATAM that guarantees your team can maintain every system we build, with no vendor lock-in."

---

## 7. Proof

**What to evaluate:** Is there evidence that the company delivers what it promises? Absence of proof is a buyer objection factory.

**Questions to ask:**
- Are there client logos, case studies, testimonials, or results data?
- Is the proof specific enough to be credible? (Numbers, named clients, outcomes)
- Is proof visible above the fold, or hidden below?
- Does proof match the ICP? (Enterprise logos don't help if selling to SMEs)

**Common failure modes:**
- No proof at all (most common for pre-launch)
- Generic testimonials ("Great team to work with!")
- Proof from the wrong ICP
- Proof that's vague ("improved efficiency") without numbers

**What good looks like:**
- "Reduced invoice processing time by 73% for [Client Name], a 400-person logistics company in São Paulo."

---

## 8. Enemy / alternative

**What to evaluate:** Great positioning names what the buyer is doing now (the enemy) and explains why this is better.

**Questions to ask:**
- What is the buyer currently doing to solve this problem? (Manual work? Excel? Another vendor? Nothing?)
- Does the company positioning itself against a named enemy?
- Is the enemy a platform, a behavior, or a mindset?
- Does the copy help the buyer feel the cost of the current approach?

**Common failure modes:**
- Positioning against nothing — "We offer AI solutions" doesn't tell buyers what to leave
- Attacking direct competitors directly (risky and usually unnecessary)
- Missing the real enemy: often "internal team builds it" or "we'll just use ChatGPT"

---

## 9. Why now

**What to evaluate:** Market timing argument. Why is this the right moment to exist?

**Questions to ask:**
- Is there a market inflection point supporting this company's existence? (LLM commoditization, AI adoption wave, LATAM digital maturity)
- Is the "why now" stated or implied?
- Does the company benefit from favorable timing or fight against it?

---

## 10. Why this team

**What to evaluate:** Is the founding team or company background credible for this specific work?

**Questions to ask:**
- Are there bios, credentials, or signals that explain why these people are uniquely positioned to do this work?
- Does the team page (if it exists) build or destroy credibility?
- Is there a domain-specific proof point? (Ex-Google AI team, 50 deployments, university partnership)
- Does the team look like it could actually deliver what the homepage promises?

---

## 11. Why not do nothing

**What to evaluate:** The strongest competitor is always inaction. Does the copy make doing nothing feel risky?

**Questions to ask:**
- Does the copy articulate the cost of not acting?
- Is the cost of inaction immediate/vivid or distant/abstract?
- Does the company make a case for urgency tied to competitive pressure or compounding risk?
- Would a buyer who reads this feel any urgency, or could they bookmark it and forget it?
