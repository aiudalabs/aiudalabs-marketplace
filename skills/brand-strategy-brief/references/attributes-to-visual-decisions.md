# From attributes to visual direction

An attribute becomes useful when it narrows the options. This guide shows how to turn one into direction for the four parts of a visual identity.

## The levers

Each part of an identity has a few levers. An attribute pushes each lever one way or the other.

| Part | Levers |
| --- | --- |
| Color | Hue family · saturation (muted to vivid) · lightness contrast (soft to stark) · neutral temperature (warm to cool) · number of colors (few to many) |
| Typography | Structure (geometric, humanist, grotesque, serif, slab, mono) · weight contrast (even to dramatic) · width (condensed to wide) · case (sentence case to all caps) · scale ratio (gentle to steep) |
| Shape and layout | Corners (sharp to round) · density (tight to airy) · alignment (strict grid to loose) · depth (flat to layered) |
| Imagery | Type (photo, illustration, product, abstract) · subject (people, objects, data) · treatment (natural to stylized) |

## How to translate

For each attribute:

1. Ask what a person would have to see to believe the attribute without being told.
2. Push two or three levers, not all of them. An identity that pushes every lever the same way becomes a caricature.
3. Write the direction as a range and an exclusion.

### Worked example

Attribute: **precise**. Meaning here: "we measure before we claim". Rules out: anything decorative or approximate.

| Part | Direction | Excludes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Color | Few colors; one accent used only for data and actions; stark lightness contrast | Gradients, more than one accent |
| Typography | Grotesque or mono for numbers; tabular figures; gentle scale ratio | Script, display faces, tight letterspacing on body text |
| Shape and layout | Strict grid, sharp or barely rounded corners, flat | Organic shapes, drop shadows |
| Imagery | Product and data, shown as they are | Stock photography, abstract 3D |

Attribute: **warm**, in the same brand. Meaning here: "written by people for people". Rules out: cold corporate distance.

| Part | Direction | Excludes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Color | Neutrals tinted warm; off-white surface instead of pure white | Pure grey, pure black text |
| Typography | Humanist details in the body face; sentence case | All-caps headings |
| Shape and layout | Generous spacing | Dense tables as the first thing seen |
| Imagery | Real people from the team or the customer base | Staged handshakes |

Notice the tension: "precise" asks for stark contrast, "warm" asks for off-white and softened black. That tension is the identity. Resolve it explicitly, for example "stark contrast in data views, warm neutrals everywhere else", and write the resolution in the brief.

## Do not over-constrain

The exclusions from three attributes add up. If together they remove color, emphasis, contrast and warmth, what is left is correct and lifeless. After writing the table, read the "Excludes" column as one list. If it reads like a ban on anything expressive, cut it back until at least one source of warmth and one moment of contrast survive.

The direction here feeds the next step, where whole pages are compared by eye. It is guidance for those pages, not a specification.

## Checks

- Each direction can reject a real option. "Clean and modern" rejects nothing.
- No hex values and no font names yet. Those are chosen later, against this direction.
- Conflicts between attributes are resolved in writing, not left for whoever designs the first screen.
- The direction respects the follow-or-break decisions from the audit.
